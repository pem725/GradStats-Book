10  Validity (not Reliability)

Reliability asked whether our sensor is consistent. Validity asks the harder, more important question: is it measuring what we think it is measuring? A scale that reliably reads five pounds heavy is useless; a reaction-time task that “measures attention” but actually measures how fast someone can move a finger will send a whole literature down the wrong road. Validity is where measurement meets meaning, and it is never a single number - it is an argument you build.

10.1 Learning Objectives

  1. Distinguish validity (on target) from reliability (consistent).
  2. Know the forms of validity that concern a measure.
  3. Know the forms of validity that concern an inference or study.
  4. Understand why validity is an accumulating argument, not a one-time test.

10.2 Validity of the Measure

The first family of validity questions is about the measure itself - does this instrument capture the construct it claims to?

  1. Face validity - does it look right on the surface? The weakest form (appearances deceive), but not nothing: a measure that looks absurd will not be trusted or used.
  2. Content validity - do the items cover the whole construct, not just a convenient slice? A math test that only asks about addition has poor content validity for “math ability.”
  3. Criterion validity - does the measure correlate with an external benchmark (a criterion) it should? Two timings:
    • Concurrent - agrees with a criterion measured now (a new depression scale tracks a clinician’s diagnosis today).
    • Predictive - forecasts a criterion in the future (the SAT predicting college GPA).
  4. Construct validity - the big one, the umbrella over all the others: does the measure behave the way the theory of the construct says it should? This is assembled from two complementary pieces:
    • Convergent validity - it correlates with things it should (a new anxiety scale agrees with established anxiety scales).
    • Discriminant validity - it does not correlate with things it should not (that anxiety scale is not just re-measuring general negative mood or verbal ability).
# A cartoon of convergent vs discriminant validity as a correlation matrix
set.seed(4)
anxiety_true <- rnorm(300)
new_scale <- anxiety_true + rnorm(300, 0, 0.5)          # our new measure
old_scale <- anxiety_true + rnorm(300, 0, 0.5)          # established anxiety measure
verbal    <- rnorm(300)                                  # unrelated construct
round(cor(data.frame(new_scale, old_scale, verbal)), 2)
          new_scale old_scale verbal
new_scale      1.00      0.80   0.05
old_scale      0.80      1.00   0.05
verbal         0.05      0.05   1.00

We want the top-left block (new vs. old anxiety) to be high - convergent - and the correlations with verbal to be near zero - discriminant. A measure earns construct validity by passing both tests at once.

10.3 Validity of the Inference

The second family is broader and, for a working scientist, even more consequential. These validities are not about a single instrument but about whether the conclusions of a whole study hold up. (This framework comes from the Campbell–Shadish–Cook tradition of research design.)

  1. Statistical conclusion validity - is there really a relationship, and did we estimate it correctly? Threatened by low power, violated assumptions, and the fishing expeditions we warned about in the hypothesis-testing chapter.
  2. Internal validity - can we claim X actually caused Y, rather than some lurking third variable? This is what randomized experiments buy you, and what correlational studies cannot.
  3. External validity - do the results generalize - to other people, settings, times, and versions of the treatment? A finding true only of sophomores in one lab in one year is real but narrow.
  4. Construct validity (of the study) - are the operations we ran (this manipulation, this measure) faithful stand-ins for the constructs we theorize about? It is the same construct-validity idea, now aimed at the whole design rather than one scale.

These four often trade off. Tightening internal validity (a controlled lab experiment) frequently costs external validity (the lab is not the world). Recognizing which validity you are buying - and which you are sacrificing - is the mark of a mature researcher.

ImportantReliability and Validity, Side by Side

Return to the dartboard. Reliability is a tight cluster (consistent). Validity is a cluster on the bullseye (accurate). You can be:

  • Reliable but not valid - tight cluster in the wrong corner (the scale that always reads five pounds heavy). Dangerous, because the consistency feels like trustworthiness.
  • Valid but not reliable - centered on the bullseye but scattered wide. Rare in practice, since heavy noise usually drags accuracy down too.
  • Neither - scattered and off-target. At least it is obviously bad.
  • Both - tight and centered. The goal.

Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity. A consistent measure of the wrong thing is still a measure of the wrong thing.

10.4 Validity Is an Argument, Not a Verdict

There is no single test, no magic coefficient, that stamps a measure “VALID.” Validity is a case you build over time, from many studies and many kinds of evidence - convergent here, predictive there, a failed discriminant test forcing a revision. It is provisional and cumulative, exactly like science itself. Treat any claim of validity as an ongoing argument that new evidence can strengthen or overturn.

NoteFrom the Field

Reconceiving what a measure captures is real research, not a philosophy seminar. Coan, Allen, and McKnight (Coan et al. 2006) argued that frontal EEG asymmetry is better understood as a capability than a fixed trait - a validity argument that changed how the measure is interpreted. And McKnight and Sechrest (McKnight and Sechrest 2003) traced what goes wrong when a construct as basic as “experience” is invoked for decades without ever being measured well. The deepest measurement questions are validity questions.

10.5 Challenge

TipDo One Yourself
  1. Pick a construct you care about (grit, burnout, reading ability). Sketch one concrete piece of evidence you would collect for its content, convergent, discriminant, and predictive validity.
  2. Extend the correlation-matrix demo: add a second unrelated construct and confirm your new scale shows discriminant validity against both. What correlation pattern would make you doubt the scale?
  3. Describe a study that is high in internal validity but low in external validity, and one that is the reverse. What did each buy, and what did each give up?

10.6 Where We Go Next

We now have trustworthy sensors - reliable and valid - feeding us clean data. At last we can build the machines that turn that data into inference. The unifying framework for all of them is the General Linear Model.
