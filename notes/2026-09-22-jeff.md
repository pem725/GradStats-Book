# What Pat and Jeff decided, 2026-09-22

Distilled from the 80,000-character recording of the meeting. Most of it was not about
the book; these are the parts that are.

## The fact that changes the stakes

**Jeff is teaching from the published book right now.** In his own words he has been
sending students to it — "I recommended the online book for the distributions and
transformations [chapter]… it was spot on for all my notes. I recommend students go up
and read that chapter."

He does not assign it by chapter number; he points at a topic. So a defect on the live
site is classroom material this semester, not a cosmetic problem. That reorders the open
list: the ten raw PSPP errors still published across `15-MRC`, `21-graphics`,
`20-beyond` and `30-irt` are being read by students.

## The three things a stats course has to leave behind

Both of them, independently, on what an undergraduate should still have a year later:

1. **Variance**
2. **Null-hypothesis significance testing**
3. **Reliability**

Jeff: *"If they left the class really understanding variance, understanding null
hypothesis significance testing, understanding the fundamentals of reliability… as a
graduate professor, taking students like that, it would have been so easy."*

Against that: the standard syllabus is *"a fire hose of factoids"* — Kruskal-Wallis,
Mann-Whitney, MANOVA, data reduction, taught as separate machinery. Their position is
that the rank tests deserve **one slide** showing each as the parallel of a linear-model
procedure already covered, and no more. The book already argues this; the meeting was
the two of them agreeing it is the right call.

## The key pieces — concepts to hand to *other* faculty

The concrete thing they want built. Not more stats teaching, but short drop-in pieces
that content-course instructors can use, because the ideas do not survive when they live
only in a stats class.

Jeff: *"I have talked about each of those different things from time to time in stats
class, but it doesn't stand. It doesn't work in there."*

Named so far:

- **The counterfactual.**
- **Conditioning on the consequence.** Pat: *"If we don't drum that into students, we
  need to drum it into faculty, because the faculty are the worst at it."*

Shape they agreed on: **a three-slide template per concept**, plus one worked example
in the instructor's own subject area, so it can be dropped into a content course
without the instructor having to invent the example. Pat's phrase for the endpoint was
a **"central repository for statistical ideas."**

Status: named, not written. This is the main new piece of work out of the meeting.

## A graphing example they want built

Jeff does not teach graphing as a topic — *"it's not a class, you don't need a class to
show you how to graph something. Here are two rules: pay attention to what the axes
are."*

What he does want is **one example of a graph that is genuinely useful and misleading at
the same time**, built from a real frequency table with tiny cell counts. He described
one with five cases where every value had a frequency of one. The pedagogical point is
the gap between a technically correct picture and a true one.

Candidate home: the [graphics chapter](../21-graphics.qmd), or the tables chapter, which
already argues about choosing digits on purpose.

## Smaller notes

- Jeff teaches the one-sample *z* and *t* only because they are easy on-ramps, not
  because they matter.
- He wants the linear model foregrounded because *"you are going to see this in SEM, you
  are going to see this in all sorts of model testing"* — the same spine the book uses.
- Course-evaluation G-theory study: instructor main effect explains very little variance;
  class type and structure explain a lot, and the two are confounded. Unweighted response
  rates are a known gap. Not book work, but it is the authors' own data and a candidate
  example for the generalizability material in `30-irt`.
